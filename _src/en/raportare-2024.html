1. Living lab implementation framework

A. Quality and risk mitigation plan

The project's objectives are: (1) to set up pilot living labs in 4 European cities, showing how sustainable urban agriculture can empower and include vulnerable groups, and promoting the transition to low-impact, regenerative urban food systems that provide healthy food for all residents, especially low-income consumers; (2) to work directly with authorities, communities and local actors on field demonstrations of sustainable agriculture, waste valorisation, short supply chains and primary processing; (3) to identify barriers and levers for uptake and provide tangible evidence for decision-makers, communities, innovators and entrepreneurs; and (4) to identify relevant scientific, social and technical indicators and collect data in each living lab to feed the central knowledge hub.

The quality management plan sets out how partners cooperate and share information, covering project management, data management, quality and risk management for the whole project. It is a living document, updated as needed in line with Driving Urban Transitions (DUT) reporting guidelines and national funding agency requirements. The risk management strategy aims to identify potential risks early, assign owners and mitigation measures, and also to recognise and exploit opportunities.

B. Data management plan

Data to be collected covers four areas: sustainable food systems (vegetable area grown for consumption, number of local producers and markets, consumer surveys on access to local produce); a pesticide-use index; agricultural employment (employment rate, new hires, retraining); and an economic database (number of firms, employees, turnover, profit). Data is collected annually from external sources, stored as CSV and Excel files, and larger volumes are organised in MySQL databases and a geographic information system.

For data management, the team purchased laptops, back-up hard drives and office supplies, and was finalising the purchase of a data-processing server that will run AI applications and support the project dashboard.

C. Dissemination strategy and plan

The aim is to make all project results available to key stakeholders through an inclusive, participatory approach that draws on partners' expertise, with digital and social channels at its core. Key objectives include engaging local communities and public authorities, promoting the multi-actor capacity-building programme, supporting FEED4FOOD's sustainability and replicability, promoting the data framework of the central knowledge hub, and building a strong network of FEED4FOOD cities.

The plan has three phases: awareness (months 1–12), pan-European outreach and engagement (months 13–36), and pan-European exploitation and replication (months 26–36).

2. Community needs assessment

Fresh vegetable production is essential for sustainable agriculture and food security, especially in urban and peri-urban areas such as Bucharest-Ilfov. The assessment looked at how food reaches consumers: Romania is integrated into European and global retail networks, and the number of foreign-owned store chains grew strongly after 2000. With a population of 19,053,815 at the latest census, Romania has about 4,567 inhabitants per store. The report also looks at smaller Romanian-owned supermarket networks, farmers' markets and seasonal fairs, and greenhouse production, which reaches the market earlier at higher prices.

Maps produced for the study area show the evolution of four economic indicators in agriculture: number of firms, number of employees, turnover and profit.

3. Literature review

Urban expansion has a significant impact on land use, converting farmland and natural areas, affecting biodiversity and ecosystems. It is driven largely by economic growth, and its impact varies between world regions. Urban expansion and agricultural living labs are linked through the use of urban and peri-urban land: living labs are innovative platforms that support sustainable agriculture by bringing together diverse stakeholders, including citizens, to develop and test new practices and technologies in real-world conditions. They can strengthen capacity-building, contribute to the UN Sustainable Development Goals and are valuable tools for innovation in sustainable agriculture.

4. Existing models and initiatives

Similar living labs exist in Romania at the West University of Timișoara (UVT Digital and Green Living Lab), Alexandru Ioan Cuza University of Iași (Research & Innovation for Cities and Citizens) and the Politehnica University of Bucharest (Education 4.0 Living Labs). The review also covers living labs adapted to Central and Eastern Europe, rural living labs addressing depopulation and economic decline, Australian living labs used as quasi-experiments for open innovation, and German living labs for nature-based solutions. Common challenges include governance, efficiency, participant recruitment and impact assessment; long-term sustainability often depends on viable business models.
